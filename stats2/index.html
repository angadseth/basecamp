01

What this course actually asks

From describing to concluding

Statistics 2 is taught by Andrew Thangaraj of the electrical engineering department at IIT Madras, and it is taught unusually: from data first. Casino odds, IPL scorecards, height and weight tables, with Colab illustrations alongside the derivations. If Statistics 1 felt abstract, this one will feel less so, not more.

The content is the real payload though. Weeks 1 to 5 extend probability to several variables at once and arrive at the limit theorems. Weeks 7 to 11 are inference — estimation, Bayesian estimation, and hypothesis testing. That is the first time the programme asks you to go from a sample to a claim about the world, with a stated chance of being wrong.

This is the course the Diploma quietly assumes. Estimators, likelihood, confidence and testing are the vocabulary of Machine Learning Foundations. Along with Maths 2, it is the most valuable Foundation course to actually understand rather than pass.

It has the heaviest requisite list in Foundation: Statistics 1 and Maths 1 must be done, and Maths 2 is a co-requisite — which is why the suggested pathway puts it last.

In one line

Several random variables at once, the limit theorems, and then inference.

Book

Probability and Statistics with Examples using R by Athreya, Sarkar and Tanner — free online, linked below. Two reference volumes on joint distributions come from the course page.

03

Week 5, run rather than stated

Why inference works at all

Pick a source distribution — the uglier the better — and raise the sample size. The green bars are four thousand sample means actually computed; the purple curve is the normal distribution the limit theorem predicts. Start at n = 1, where they disagree completely, and watch what happens by n = 30.

The distribution of the sample mean

4,000 trials, recomputed live

the means you actually got what the theorem predicts

Two things are happening at once. The shape becomes normal — that is the central limit theorem. And the spread shrinks like σ/√n — that is why quadrupling your sample only halves your error, and why every confidence interval in week 8 has a square root in it.

How it is graded

May 2026 grading document · confirm against your course page

The standard formula, plus the same bonus structure as Statistics 1:

T = max( 0.6F + 0.3·max(Qz1, Qz2),  0.45F + 0.25Qz1 + 0.3Qz2 )
    + bonus for extra activity, capped at 5

The five bonus marks

Five extra activities across the term — in May 2026 they ran from week 1 onward, with a submission deadline and then a separate peer-review deadline about two weeks later. Worth 5 marks total: 3.75 for the activity and 1.25 for quality, at the instructors' discretion.

Five peer reviews, or the activity scores zero

The rule is identical to Statistics 1 and catches people just as often: submit the activity but complete fewer than five peer reviews and you are awarded 0 for it. The reviews have their own deadline, after the submission deadline — put both in your calendar.

The eligibility gate

Average of the best 5 of 8 — the first seven weekly assignments plus Mock Quiz 1 — must be 40/100 or more, and you must attend at least one quiz.

Bonus marks only if you pass

The five marks are added to T after you have passed. They move a grade boundary; they do not rescue a fail.

05

Where the marks actually go

My read, from this course and from what people ask about
01

Treating week 6 as a holiday

The refresher week exists because the course knows the second half is harder. Spending it consolidating weeks 1–5 is worth more than any single week of new content.

02

Reading the p-value backwards

A p-value is the probability of data at least this extreme if the null hypothesis is true — not the probability that the null is true. Weeks 10 and 11 are written to catch the reversal.

03

Independence assumed, not checked

From week 2 onward, half the formulas only hold if the variables are independent. Questions supply dependent pairs on purpose. Check before you multiply.

04

Forgetting the peer reviews

Same as Statistics 1: five marks forfeited for a step that takes fifteen minutes and has its own separate deadline.

What to do in week one

Term starts 2 October 2026
  1. Find the five activity deadlinesBoth dates for each — submission, and then peer review. Five marks, and the most commonly forfeited five in Foundation.
  2. Make sure Stats 1 is solidExpectation, variance and conditional probability are assumed from lecture one, not re-taught.
  3. Open a ColabThe lectures use one. Following along with the IPL and height–weight data is the difference between watching and learning.
  4. Do not coast to week 6Week 5 is the densest in the course. If it is shaky, the refresher week is your one chance to fix it before inference starts.
  5. Plan for weeks 7–11 outside the gateAll of inference sits after eligibility closes, and it is most of the end term.
